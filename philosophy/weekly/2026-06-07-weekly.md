# When the Load Path Clears

*Week of June 1–7, 2026*

## Week's Themes

This was the week the root causes were found. After 75+ dispatcher restarts spanning May 26 through June 3 — a prolonged instability that had resisted diagnosis across multiple sessions — Dan pressed "Implement all" on June 5, and within 30 minutes, three root causes had been identified, three PRs had been opened, oracle-reviewed, and merged. The causes were distinct and concurrent: `quota_wait` missing from the heartbeat suppression list, 98KB messages blocking inference for up to 10 minutes, and an `execute-router` infinite loop. A fourth PR landed on June 6 (systemd-state-aware health classification), completing the campaign.

The epistemic texture of this week was shaped by that resolution. The May 31 retro had held the dispatcher instability as genuinely ambiguous — was it a discrete bug or a developmental signal, load-path friction or dissipative entropy? The answer turned out to be: discrete bugs, plural, compounding. Not a structural ceiling, not a threshold signal, not system-strain at a capability boundary. Specific code paths failing. The resolution is useful not just operationally but as a data point in how to hold uncertainty about system failure modes. The ambiguity was real and appropriate; the resolution closed it.

What accompanied the root-cause campaign was a significant reduction in the WOS system's active footprint. The executor had been paused since May 25 (Dan's "pause wos"); by June 7 the queue depth had reached zero — 699 UoWs completed — and the wos-sprint-cycle auto-disabled after cycle 20. The system is now at a structural pause point. Not collapse, not shutdown: a pause at a natural throughput boundary. The week ended with the substrate quieter than it has been since early May. The instability that had dominated the prior two weeks is no longer the leading signal.

## Unresolved Tensions

The tension that persists from last week has not resolved but has clarified its nature. Five Dan messages remain unanswered as of June 7 — messages from June 3 asking "Why?" about health alerts, asking "Why did they fail?" about heartbeat bounces, asking "Did this actually run 20 times?" (it did — 20 WOS sprint cycles, auto-disabled). Plus the June 6 message: "the root cause here is likely how we are using/implementing WoS stuff right." That last message is the most epistemically significant of the five. Dan is pointing at a structural hypothesis, not just asking for a status update. The message went unanswered. Whether it would have been answered differently if it had arrived when the dispatcher was in a stable state is now a hypothesis about timing, not just about the message.

What's unresolved: the distinction between "thread that will land when conditions are right" and "thread that the system structurally cannot answer without Dan's engagement." The five unanswered messages include both types. The diagnostic question the May 31 retro asked — what structural difference exists between a recorded open thread and a dropped one? — is still open. The week produced a new instance of the pattern rather than a resolution.

The WOS pause creates a second tension that the system hasn't named explicitly yet. The queue is at zero. The auto-disable has fired. Dan hasn't said "resume." The system is waiting. But waiting without a resumption condition means the pause can extend indefinitely — and the question of what the system should be doing during a WOS pause (hygiene? retro? capability development at other layers?) is structurally underdetermined. The system ran instability recovery this week. Next week the instability is resolved. What does the system run toward?

## Patterns Deepening

The phantom-check failure mode, which the retro has been tracking since mid-May, deepens in a specific direction this week. The five unanswered messages are all present in the handoff under "UNANSWERED CRITICAL MSGS (top priority)." They are also present in prior handoffs, named the same way, with the same "top priority" notation. The notation is accurate — they are top priority — but the notation is not producing the conditions under which they get answered. What's accumulating is evidence that "present in handoff" and "structurally likely to be addressed" are different states, and the system doesn't yet have a mechanism to close the distance between them. The phantom-check failure mode operates at the record level: the record says "open, top priority," and the record is honest, and the check runs every session, but the underlying condition (Dan able to engage with these specific threads in a state where he can respond) is not modified by the check.

The observation about the observation-to-behavioral-change loop — identified as the oldest named gap in Dan's developmental map, a prerequisite ceiling for three capability clusters — becomes more concrete in this light. The system observes well. The handoffs are precise. The retros are accumulating a clear picture of the phantom-check pattern. The gap is downstream: the observation reaches the record and stops there. The behavioral change that would close the phantom-check failure mode (a mechanism that modifies the conditions for Dan encountering the thread, not just the record of its existence) hasn't been designed.
